# REBIRTH GAME - SAKYA PANDITA (1)
"Rebirth the Tibetan Game of Liberation"

Three versions (interchangeable) 

board at:

This Tibetan board game, teaching the basic principles of Buddhism, was created by Sakya Pandita Kunga Gyaltsen (1182-1251) to entertain his ailing mother while she was recovering from an illness. 

To keep his mother awake and mentally active during the day, prevent her from sleeping excessively

1 -> 104 squares on a board. "Represents 
* everything almost from the wheel of life, 
* [...] the Buddhist path and its various goals [...], as well as 
* the different Buddha realms of the 5 symbolic Buddhists / 5 Tathāgathas:
1. Vairochana, 
2. Amitabha, 
3. Akshobhya, 
4. Ratnasambhava, and 
5. Amoghasiddhi

* Different visual model of moving through Samsara

The game is filled with connections

Via (EXPLANATION OF THE GAME)[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P_iWvO8dWxI]

BOOK REFERENCE:
Rebirth, The Tibetan Game of Liberation. Mark Tatz and Jody Kent. Rider & Company, London 1978.

RULES
* Each player starts with a token on the opening square of the game
* Move around the board based on the roll of the dice. 

* The lower squares where a player can land represent the lower realms of existence such as hell and the ghost realms. 
* The upper squares on the board are the various pure lands, buddha realms, the ten bodhisattva bhumis and finally enlightenment. 

* It is claimed by some Tibetan informants that the Rebirth Game is sometimes used as a form of divination.

# MILTON'S CHECKERED GAME OF LIFE - 1860
at boards/Milton Checkered Game of Life.jpg

The design of Milton Bradley’s first game was a simple checkerboard, a familiar form to most people, with alternating red and white squares in an eight-by-eight grid. The red squares were neutral and the white squares contained words representing good or bad values or conditions, from honesty or perseverance or truth to idleness or crime or ruin. Landing on a constructive trait or situation would send the player forward, advancing his positive path through life, whereas landing on a place of vice would impede a player’s progress.

Paramapadam (Snakes and Ladders) is a traditional board game. Played by two or more people, the game board consists of a grid of square cells. Typically, the board features a grid of 8×8, 10×10, or 12×12 squares. Certain squares are connected by ladders and snakes. The number and arrangement of the ladders and snakes may vary from one board to another.[1]

History
This game was played in ancient India around the 2nd century BCE. It was introduced in England around 1892. The game may have been introduced as a means to illustrate to children the consequences of good deeds and bad deeds.[2]

Vaikunta Ekadasi
“Traditionally, Parama Pada Sopanam is played on the night of Vaikuntha Ekadashi (the 11th day after the new moon in the Tamil month of Margazhi). Many Hindus believe that the door to Vaikuntha, the abode of Lord Vishnu, will be wide open to welcome the devout and the faithful. Hindus also believe that dying on Vaikuntha Ekadashi will take them directly to the abode of Vishnu, liberating them from the cycle of rebirth. On this day, the devout stay up all night fasting and praying and playing the game helps them pass the time till dawn, when the fast is broken.”

Gameplay
This game involves moving game pieces by rolling a die. It can be played by two or more people. No special skills are required to play it. Generally, to start the game, a player must roll a "1" on the die. Subsequently, players take turns rolling the die and moving their pieces according to the number rolled. A piece landing at the foot of a ladder ascends to the top of the ladder, while a piece landing at the mouth of a snake slides down to the snake's tail. The piece that successfully navigates these obstacles and reaches the final square wins the game.

# SUFI VERSION - 1915
at boards/Sufi Version.png

Usually comprising 100 squares inscribed in Persian, the playing area conducts the players hazardously upward from lower spiritual states to the final goal of heaven, according to the throw of dice and sudden demotions or promotions through snakes or ladders. Most surviving examples are held in British collections, including that of the Royal Asiatic Society. Detailed attention is given to a unique, expanded version of the standard Sufi board which came to light a few years ago. Innovative and elaborate in its structure, method of play and nomenclature, it seems however to have been a late and short-lived experiment.
